What you'd give up

Trace is narrower than any one of these tools on purpose. Here's what it doesn't do, so you know what to keep.

  • Watching one customer's session. If support replays a specific customer's visit to answer a ticket, keep a replay tool for that. Trace is for the patterns.
  • Your backend's errors. Trace sees what breaks in the browser, not server exceptions, stack traces from your services or on-call alerts. Keep your error tracker for those.
  • Open-ended analytics. Trace measures the goals, funnels and areas you declare. Ad-hoc cohorts by any property, warehouse exports and years of history stay with your analytics tool.
  • Heatmap images. Clicks are counted per element and shown as tables, not painted over a screenshot.
  • Surveys and feedback widgets. Trace doesn't ask visitors anything.
  • Your history. Trace starts counting from its first visit; there's no import. That's why you'd run it beside your tools for a few weeks.

Questions teams ask before switching

Will another script slow the site down?

Trace's collector is 8.3 KB, only takes notes while the page is busy, and does its work when the browser is idle. A test fails our build if it grows past that.

Do we need a coding agent?

No. The dashboard finds and ranks problems on its own, and every insight is a task you can hand to a developer. An agent makes the setup and the fix faster.

We already have a consent tool. Does Trace need it?

By default Trace stores nothing on the device. If your policy is to load nothing before consent anyway, switch it to wait for your consent tool.
